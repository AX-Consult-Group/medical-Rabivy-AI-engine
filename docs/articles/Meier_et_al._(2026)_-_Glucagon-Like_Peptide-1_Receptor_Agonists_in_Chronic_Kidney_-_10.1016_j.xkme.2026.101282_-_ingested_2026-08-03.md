# Meier et al. (2026) - Glucagon-Like Peptide-1 Receptor Agonists in Chronic Kidney Disease


## Meier et al. (2026): INTRODUCTION TO GLP-1 RECEPTOR AGONISTS


### p1

Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists are incretin-based therapies that were initially developed for managing type 2 diabetes. These agents enhance insulin secretion in a glucose-dependent manner and inhibit glucagon release, which helps control hyperglycemia without inducing significant hypoglycemia. 1 Additionally, GLP-1 receptor agonists promote weight loss by reducing appetite and caloric intake through hypothalamic pathways. 2 According to DeFronzo, these drugs address multiple defects involved in the pathophysiology of type 2 diabetes, which he referred to as the "Ominous Octet."

### p2

DeFronzo's "Ominous Octet" identifies 8 defects that contribute to the development of type 2 diabetes, and GLP-1 receptor agonists effectively address several of these. 3 They improve insulin sensitivity in muscles and liver, reduce hepatic glucose production, and enhance insulin secretion from β-cells. 1,3 Additionally, GLP-1 receptor agonists help mitigate lipotoxicity by reducing circulating fatty acids and restoring the incretin effect, which is impaired in diabetes. 2 This broad action on multiple metabolic defects makes GLP-1 receptor agonists essential for comprehensive diabetes management.

## Meier et al. (2026): Expansion Beyond Diabetes


### p1

Beyond their role in glycemic control, GLP-1 receptor agonists have significant cardiovascular benefits. GLP-1 receptor agonists improve lipid profiles, reduce inflammation, and offer protection against cardiovascular events, which are especially important for diabetic patients who are at high risk for heart disease. GLP-1 receptor agonists also reduce cardiovascular risk by promoting weight loss and lowering adiposity-related comorbidities. 2 Experimental studies have shown that GLP-1 receptors are expressed in the renal vasculature, 4 including the afferent arteriole, 5 and are predominantly expressed in proximal tubular cells. [6][7][8] This receptor distribution suggests that GLP-1 receptor agonists may contribute to kidney function. Emerging evidence supports the role of GLP-1 receptor agonists in chronic kidney disease (CKD), particularly for patients with diabetes. These agents may slow CKD progression by improving glycemic control, reducing albuminuria, and possibly protecting against glomerular damage through their anti-inflammatory effects. GLP-1 receptor agonists are also thought to improve endothelial function and reduce oxidative stress, mechanisms that are crucial in preserving kidney function and reducing the risk of kidney failure in diabetic patients. 9 Therefore, these agents are becoming an important part of the management strategy for diabetic patients with CKD. However, their role in nondiabetic CKD is less clear and is not well established. This review will explore the possible mechanisms through which GLP-1 receptor agonists exert the renoprotective effects, focusing on their direct actions on the kidney.

## Meier et al. (2026): PATHOPHYSIOLOGY OF CKD


### p1

CKD is a progressive condition where kidney function gradually declines. In the early stages, hyperfiltration occurs, where the remaining nephrons work harder to compensate for the loss of function. However, this eventually leads to further damage and glomerulosclerosis. As the disease progresses, several molecular mechanisms drive worsening kidney function.

### p2

As CKD progresses, tubulointerstitial fibrosis becomes more prominent, driven by inflammation and oxidative stress. 10 In general, oxidative stress and inflammation are closely interconnected and serve as key features of CKD, and they both contribute to CKD progression. The accumulation of reactive oxygen species exacerbates oxidative stress and triggers inflammatory pathways and growth factor production, leading to cell damage and kidney dysfunction. [11][12][13] Inflammatory molecules, such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), promote fibrosis and kidney tissue damage, and they are closely linked to a decline in kidney function. [14][15][16] Additionally, growth factors such as transforming growth factor-β contribute to scar tissue formation and fibroblast transformation into myofibroblasts, a key cellular mechanism driving fibrosis. 17 Evidence strongly supports that increased oxidative stress contributes to endothelial dysfunction, aggravating renal disease. As the disease advances, endothelial dysfunction develops, impairing blood flow to the kidneys. This increases vascular resistance, which further reduces kidney perfusion and function. It also contributes to hypertension and the presence of proteinuria, which are signs of kidney damage. 13,17 CKD also disrupts fluid and electrolyte homeostasis. As kidney function declines, the body struggles to maintain fluid balance, leading to fluid retention and imbalances in electrolytes, including potassium, sodium, and calcium. This creates a vicious cycle in which declining kidney function exacerbates imbalances and fluid overload, leading to vascular damage, nephron injury, and accelerated CKD progression. 18 The renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) becomes overactive in CKD, further exacerbating kidney damage. RAAS overactivation leads to the excessive release of angiotensin II, which causes vasoconstriction, promotes fibrosis, and increases blood pressure. These effects worsen kidney function and contribute to CKD progression. 10 Understanding these mechanisms is essential for developing effective strategies to manage CKD and slow its progression. This review will explore how GLP-1 agonists may modulate these pathways, offering renoprotective effects in CKD.

## Meier et al. (2026): GLP AGONIST RENOPROTECTIVE MECHANISMS


### p1

Global genetic deletion of GLP-1 receptor in mice led to kidney injury as evidenced by the increase in urinary albumin-creatine ratio and a decrease in plasma cystatin C, an observation that contrasts with its usual increase in kidney injury. Yet this paradoxical reduction alongside albuminuria may indicate selective glomerular dysfunction. 19 Additionally, loss of GLP-1 receptor increased fibronectin deposition and glomerular scarring, hallmark features of CKD. Moreover, kidney injury was exacerbated in diabetic conditions. 19 In line with the aforementioned study, Zhao et al 20 demonstrated that protein and mRNA expression levels of GLP-1 receptor were reduced in diabetic and nondiabetic mice with CKD. These findings highlight the importance of GLP-1 signaling in preserving kidney function with or without diabetes. Furthermore, studies in obese mice have demonstrated that semaglutide can alleviate high-fat diet-induced glomerular and tubular injury, as evidenced by hematoxylin and eosin, Masson's trichrome, and periodic acid-Schiff staining. 21 Quantitative analysis has also shown that semaglutide treatment can improve the glomerular area in these mice. 22 These findings further emphasize the beneficial role of GLP-1 signaling in the context of kidney health and disease. It is well established in the literature that GLP-1 agonists can exert a renoprotective effect by targeting risk factors that contribute to the progression of kidney disease, including hyperglycemia and excess adiposity. 23 This review will shed light on some possible mechanisms through which GLP-receptor agonists exert their renoprotective effect through their direct action on the kidney.

## Meier et al. (2026): Receptor for Advanced Glycation End Products (RAGE)


### p1

Advanced glycation end products (AGEs) are formed through the nonenzymatic reaction of reducing sugars with proteins, lipids, or nucleic acids. By binding to their receptors (RAGE), AGEs mediate oxidative stress and inflammation, playing a significant role in various physiologic and pathologic processes. 24 Several studies have demonstrated a strong association between RAGE levels and the decline in kidney function in diabetic and nondiabetic conditions, suggesting that targeting RAGE signaling could be a therapeutic strategy to slow the progression of kidney disease. 25 Interestingly, the global deletion of the GLP-1 receptor was associated with increased RAGE protein levels in normoglycemic mice, with RAGE expression further elevated after streptozotocin injection, which is administered in the mouse model to develop type 1 diabetes. 19 The authors next investigated the protective effects of GLP-1 receptor activation in a nondiabetic animal model of CKD. Rats that underwent 5/6 nephrectomy were treated with liraglutide, a GLP-1 receptor agonist, for 7 weeks. Treatment with liraglutide resulted in a reduction in urinary albumin-creatinine ratio and urinary kidney injury molecule-1 levels, indicating improved kidney function. Additionally, liraglutide therapy reduced glomerulosclerosis and suppressed kidney RAGE protein expression, suggesting decreased inflammation. Importantly, these protective effects were not associated with changes in glucose levels, highlighting that the benefits of GLP-1 receptor agonists are independent of their glucose-lowering actions. Systolic blood pressure remained elevated in the nephrectomized rats even with liraglutide treatment. Overall, these findings demonstrate that liraglutide exerts direct renoprotective effects and can enhance kidney health in the absence of diabetes by modulating RAGE expression and reducing kidney injury. 19

## Meier et al. (2026): Reduced Sodium Reabsorption


### p1

Various clinical studies have demonstrated the contribution of GLP-1 agonists to salt and water homeostasis through their natriuretic and diuretic effects. 26,27 Experimental studies have reported similar findings, consistent with those observed in human studies. [28][29][30] The infusion of recombinant glucagon-like peptide-1 significantly increased urine output and sodium excretion in rats. This was accompanied by a reduction in both proteinuria and albuminuria. 30 Similarly, 10 μg/kg intraperitoneal injection of exendin-4, a GLP-1 receptor agonist, increased urinary water and sodium excretion in wild-type mice. These effects were absent in GLP-1 receptor-deficient mice, supporting the diuretic and natriuretic effects of GLP-1 receptor agonists. 31 Moreno et al 29 demonstrated that intravenous infusion of recombinant GLP-1 induces a dose-dependent increase in sodium and water excretion. This natriuretic effect of recombinant GLP-1 was associated with increased fractional excretion of lithium, a marker for proximal tubular reabsorption. Moreover, no significant change in renal interstitial hydrostatic pressure was observed in innervated kidney. These findings suggest that the natriuretic effect of recombinant GLP-1 is likely due to direct inhibition of sodium transport in the proximal tubule.

### p2

Rieg et al 31 showed that the natriuretic and diuretic effects of exendin-4 were associated with an increase in the phosphorylation of the Na + /H + exchanger NHE-3 at S552 and S605. The phosphorylation depends on intact GLP-1 receptor as these effects were absent in GLP-1 receptordeficient mice. In agreement with the aforementioned study, Carraro-Lacroix et al 32 showed that exendin-4 inhibits NHE-3 in proximal tubule epithelial cells derived from the kidney of a male pig. Exendin-4 inhibits NHE-3 through cAMP-dependent pathways involving both protein kinase A and the exchange protein directly activated by cAMP (EPAC), leading to increased phosphorylation of NHE-3 without affecting its surface expression. Normally, NHE-3 in the renal proximal tubule regulates sodium levels and acid-base balance by transporting sodium ions into the cell and hydrogen ions out of the cell. Therefore, inhibition of NHE-3 by exendin-4 can reduce sodium reabsorption and proton secretion, providing a renoprotective mechanism in CKD through increased sodium excretion and alleviation of fluid overload.

## Meier et al. (2026): Inflammation and Programmed Cell Death


### p1

Semaglutide, a GLP-1 receptor agonist, demonstrated renoprotective effects in an animal model of acute kidney injury. Mice that received a semaglutide injection 12 hours before the induction of ischemia-reperfusion injury exhibited improved kidney function compared with the untreated group, as evidenced by reduced serum urea and creatinine levels. Additionally, the semaglutide-treated group showed lower levels of inflammatory markers, including TNF-α, TNF-α receptor, and IL-6. 33 Notably, in an animal model of nephrotoxicity, semaglutide treatment resulted in a significant reduction in TNF-α levels, and enhanced antioxidant activity, reflected by increased glutathione levels and superoxide dismutase activity. These changes were associated with a decrease in serum creatinine, cystatin C, and urea levels and preservation of the tubular epithelial lining without notable pathological alterations. Together, these findings highlight the potential kidney-protective effects of semaglutide. 34 Semaglutide treatment prevented the decline in phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase expression. Similar findings were reported in retinal capillaries, where exendin-4 reversed the decreased levels of phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase and Akt (protein kinase B) and improved perfusion following ischemia/reperfusion injury. 35 Therefore, both studies suggest that GLP-1 agonists promote cell survival by preserving the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase/Akt signaling axis.

### p2

Semaglutide demonstrated renoprotective effects in an animal model of CKD independent of its glucose-lowering effect. Semaglutide reduced kidney inflammation and fibrosis, which was associated with reduced expression of profibrotic factors and inflammatory mediators. Moreover, the authors showed that semaglutide treatment enhanced the expression of kidney mitochondrial genes. These findings highlight the potential role of GLP-1 receptor agonists in ameliorating mitochondrial dysfunction, which has been strongly linked to the progression of CKD. 20 In a diabetic animal model, semaglutide reduced renal inflammation and fibrosis by suppressing ferroptosis, a form of programmed cell death triggered by iron accumulation and lipid peroxidation. 36 One of the signaling events involved in inhibiting ferroptosis is through the phosphorylation and activation of protein kinase A. 36 Notably, Sourris et al 19 reported that protein kinase A activity was reduced in normoglycemic mice following the deletion of the GLP-1 receptor. These findings suggest that, since GLP-1 receptor signaling regulates protein kinase A activity, GLP-1 receptor agonists may exert renoprotective effects by inhibiting ferroptosis in nondiabetic settings.

## Meier et al. (2026): Renin Angiotensin System Modulation


### p1

The nephrotoxic serum nephritis model lacks features of metabolic syndrome such as hyperglycemia, dyslipidemia, and obesity. The absence of these metabolic features makes it a suitable model to assess the renoprotective effects of liraglutide in CKD, independent of improvements in metabolic parameters such as blood glucose, blood pressure, and body weight. 37 Ougaard et al 38 showed that treating nephrotoxic serum nephritis animal models with liraglutide, a GLP-1 receptor agonist, reduced albumincreatinine ratio and improved glomerular filtration rate.

### p2

Additionally, liraglutide reduced renal fibrosis and leukocyte infiltration into the kidney. The authors showed that liraglutide treatment altered gene expression involved in angiogenesis, fibrosis, and inflammation, which play essential roles in CKD progression. These findings provide evidence that liraglutide improves kidney function directly, independent of its effects on glycemic control and weight loss.

### p3

The authors also showed that liraglutide treatment upregulated MAS1, a receptor for angiotensin (1-7), expression in the glomeruli. MAS1 is part of the protective arm of the renin angiotensin system mediating vasodilation, anti-inflammatory, and antifibrotic effects when activated. 39 Although direct evidence of MAS1 activation by GLP-1 receptor agonists in the kidney is limited, prior studies have shown that liraglutide increases the activity of the angiotensin (1-7)/MasR axis in the lung. 40 Therefore, it is plausible that GLP-1 receptor agonist treatment enhances MAS1-mediated signaling-the protective arm of the RAAS-thereby contributing to its renoprotective mechanism.

### p4

Bjørnholm et al 41 demonstrated that GLP-1 receptor activation increased renal renin gene expression and its redistribution along the renal vasculature, a phenomenon known as renin recruitment. Similar findings were reported with angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors, which are widely used in the management of CKD. 42,43 These findings suggest that modulating renin distribution may contribute to the renoprotective effects of ACE inhibitors. Likewise, the renin recruitment observed with GLP-1 receptor agonists may contribute to their renoprotective benefits independent of glucose-lowering effects. ## Meier et al. (2026): CLINICAL EVIDENCE IN CKD


### p1

The cardiovascular outcome trials LEADER, SUSTAIN-6, and SURPASS-4 were pivotal in establishing the cardiovascular safety and benefit of GLP-1 receptor agonists in patients with type 2 diabetes. [44][45][46] Although these trials were primarily designed to assess major adverse cardiovascular events, they also included secondary renal outcomes that provided early signals of potential kidney benefit. In the LEADER trial, which enrolled 9,340 patients with type 2 diabetes-approximately 23% of whom had baseline CKD-liraglutide significantly reduced the risk of a composite renal outcome (including new-onset persistent macroalbuminuria, doubling of serum creatinine, initiation of renal replacement therapy, or renal death) by 22% compared with placebo (hazard ratio [HR], 0.78; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.67-0.92). 44 Similarly, in SUSTAIN-6, which enrolled 3,297 patientsapproximately 25% of whom had estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) <60 mL/min/1.73 m 2 -semaglutide reduced the risk of a similar composite kidney endpoint by 36% (HR, 0.64; 95% CI, 0.46-0.88), primarily driven by reductions in macroalbuminuria. 45 In the SURPASS-4 trial, which compared tirzepatide to insulin glargine in >2,000 patients, a slower rate of eGFR decline was observed in the tirzepatide group. 46 It is important to recognize that tirzepatide is a dual glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide receptor and GLP-1 receptor agonist, rather than a selective GLP-1 receptor agonist. Its dual mechanism augments insulin secretion, enhances lipid metabolism, and facilitates greater weight reduction. Therefore, while studies such as SURPASS-4 and SURMOUNT-MMO offer valuable insight into the renal and cardiovascular potential of incretinbased therapy, direct comparisons with trials involving selective GLP-1 receptor agonists should be made cautiously. Nonetheless, given that many of tirzepatide's protective effects may be mediated via GLP-1 receptor signaling, these studies provide supportive, albeit indirect, evidence for the renal benefits of incretin receptor agonism. Although none of these trials were powered for renal outcomes, they offered the first clinical indications that glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide receptor/ GLP-1 receptor agonists may have meaningful renoprotective effects in patients with diabetes, especially those with existing kidney impairment.

### p2

Additional large cardiovascular outcome trials such as AMPLITUDE-O and EXSCEL have provided complementary evidence supporting renal protection with GLP-1 receptor agonists. In AMPLITUDE-O, which evaluated efpeglenatide in patients with type 2 diabetes and established cardiovascular or kidney disease, the risk of a composite kidney outcome defined as new macroalbuminuria, sustained ≥40% eGFR decline, or kidney failure, was reduced by 32% compared with placebo (HR, 0.68; 95% CI, 0.57-0.79). 47 Likewise, in EXSCEL, exenatide once weekly demonstrated a 15% reduction in the composite of new macroalbuminuria, sustained decline in eGFR, or need for renal replacement therapy. 48 Although these trials were not primarily powered for renal outcomes, the consistency of findings across multiple GLP-1 receptor agonists reinforces their potential as kidney-protective agents. ### p3

A major limitation of all prior GLP-1 receptor agonist cardiovascular outcome trials was their exclusive enrollment of patients with type 2 diabetes, leaving uncertainty about renal benefit in individuals without diabetes. This gap began to close with the secondary analysis of the SELECT trial, which enrolled 17,604 participants with overweight or obesity and established cardiovascular disease-but notably excluded individuals with diabetes. Approximately 17% of participants (n = 3,014) had CKD at baseline, defined by an eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m 2 . Although the primary endpoint of SELECT was major adverse cardiovascular events, the secondary kidney outcomes were compelling: semaglutide 2.4 mg once weekly reduced the risk of a composite renal endpoint-including sustained ≥50% decline in eGFR, kidney failure, kidney-related death, or new-onset persistent macroalbuminuria-by 22% compared with placebo (HR, 0.78; 95% CI, 0.63-0.96; P = 0.02). Semaglutide also significantly slowed the rate of eGFR decline by 0.75 mL/min/ 1.73 m 2 across the entire study population, with an even greater benefit of 2.19 mL/min/1.73 m 2 among those with baseline eGFR <60. Although these findings support the potential for GLP-1 receptor agonists to impact CKD progression even in nondiabetic individuals, the renal outcomes remained secondary, and the trial was not specifically powered to detect renal differences, limiting the strength of CKD-specific conclusions. 49 The FLOW trial, published in 2024, addressed many existing limitations by becoming the first large-scale GLP-1 receptor agonists trial to evaluate major adverse kidney events as a primary outcome. The study enrolled 3,533 patients with type 2 diabetes and high-risk CKD, randomizing them to receive once-weekly semaglutide 1.0 mg or placebo over a median follow-up of 3.4 years. Semaglutide significantly reduced the risk of the composite primary outcome-which included kidney failure, sustained ≥50% eGFR decline, or death from renal or cardiovascular causes-by 24% (HR, 0.76; 95% CI, 0.66-0.88; P = 0.0003). Importantly, the benefit was consistent across all subgroups, including baseline hemoglobin A1 c strata, reinforcing the hypothesis that semaglutide's renoprotective effects may extend beyond glycemic control. The annual rate of eGFR decline was also slower in the semaglutide group (-2.21 vs -3.03 mL/min/1.73 m 2 /y), offering strong evidence that GLP-1 receptor agonists can delay CKD progression in patients with diabetes. FLOW marked a pivotal shift in GLP-1 receptor agonist research by elevating renal outcomes to a primary endpoint and demonstrating benefit across a wide spectrum of patient characteristics. 50 The ongoing SURMOUNT-MMO trial represents a step forward in evaluating GLP-1 receptor agonists in populations without diabetes. This multinational, randomized trial is assessing the long-term cardiovascular effects of tirzepatide in individuals with obesity but without diabetes. 52 Although the primary endpoint is major adverse cardiovascular events, the trial also includes kidney outcomes as secondary endpoints, offering a valuable opportunity to further evaluate the renoprotective potential of GLP-1 receptor agonists in a nondiabetic population. However, as with the SELECT trial, the fact that major adverse kidney events are not a primary endpoint limits the strength of the conclusions that can be drawn about kidney-specific benefits.

### p4

Despite promising signals from subgroup analyses of prior trials, a dedicated renal outcomes trial in nondiabetic CKD remains an important unmet need.

## Meier et al. (2026): CURRENT LANDSCAPE OF CKD MANAGEMENT


### p1

The findings from the trials summarized above illustrate a growing body of evidence supporting the renoprotective effects of GLP-1 receptor agonists, both in patients with type 2 diabetes and in select nondiabetic populations. However, although these trials suggest meaningful benefit, particularly in slowing eGFR decline and reducing albuminuria, their place within the broader treatment landscape for nondiabetic CKD remains uncertain. Sodium/ glucose transport protein 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors, by contrast, have been extensively studied in nondiabetic CKD and are now considered a cornerstone of therapy due to their robust effects on kidney and cardiovascular outcomes. 53 To better understand where GLP-1 receptor agonists may fit in this evolving paradigm-as an alternative, an adjunct, or a subsequent option-it is important to review the key evidence supporting SGLT2 inhibitors in patients without diabetes.

### p2

Several landmark trials have firmly established the role of SGLT2 inhibitors in slowing CKD progression, with consistent benefits observed in both diabetic and nondiabetic populations. The DAPA-CKD trial, published in 2020, enrolled 4,304 patients with CKD and albuminuria (urinary albumin-creatinine ratio 200-5,000 mg/g), including 1,398 without diabetes, and demonstrated that dapagliflozin 10 mg daily significantly reduced the risk of a composite renal endpoint-sustained ≥50% decline in eGFR, end-stage kidney disease (now termed kidney failure), or death from renal or cardiovascular causes-by 39% compared with placebo (HR, 0.61; 95% CI, 0.51-0.72; P < 0.001), with similar benefit in nondiabetic participants. 54 The EMPA-KIDNEY trial, published in 2023, further expanded this evidence by enrolling over 6,600 patients with CKD (including 2,664 without diabetes) and showing that empagliflozin reduced the risk of kidney disease progression or cardiovascular death by 28% (HR, 0.72; 95% CI, 0.64-0.82; P < .001), again demonstrating consistent efficacy regardless of diabetes status. 55 Collectively, these trials demonstrate that SGLT2 inhibitors consistently slow CKD progression and reduce cardiorenal risk across a broad spectrum of patients, including those without diabetes. Beyond these pivotal outcome trials, mechanistic data provide further insight into how SGLT2 inhibitors confer kidney protection. SGLT2 inhibitors exert renoprotective effects via metabolic, hemodynamic, and direct nephroprotective mechanisms. 56 The metabolic and hemodynamic effects stem from SGLT2 inhibitors' primary mechanism of action. The inhibition of glucose reabsorption in the proximal tubule by SGLT2 inhibitors results in glucosuria, natriuresis, osmotic diuresis, and eventually systemic benefits. In contrast, the direct nephroprotective effects occur via additional pathways, including suppression of inflammatory mediators, reduction of transforming growth factor-β1 signaling and downstream fibrotic processes, enhancement of mitochondrial function and autophagic flux, reduction of oxidative stress, improved cellular repair, and podocyte protection. [57][58][59][60][61] These mechanisms support the rationale for using SGLT2 inhibitors in patients with CKD, even those without diabetes. Similarly, as discussed earlier, GLP-1 receptor agonists display renoprotective effects independent of their glucose-lowering effect through reductions in renal inflammation, fibrosis, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction.

### p3

Taken together, these trials support the use of SGLT2 inhibitors as foundational therapy in nondiabetic CKD due to their robust and consistent effects in slowing eGFR decline, reducing albuminuria, and lowering the risk of kidney failure and cardiovascular events. In contrast, although GLP-1 receptor agonists have demonstrated favorable effects on renal endpoints, the magnitude of benefit appears somewhat more modest than that observed with SGLT2 inhibitors, particularly in terms of hard renal outcomes. For example, in the SELECT trial, semaglutide 2.4 mg weekly reduced the risk of a composite kidney outcome in nondiabetic patients with obesity and cardiovascular disease by 22% (HR, 0.78; 95% CI, 0.63-0.96). 49 In contrast, the DAPA-CKD trial showed a 39% risk reduction in a similar composite outcome with dapagliflozin, and EMPA-KIDNEY reported a 28% relative risk reduction with empagliflozin, both with consistent effects in nondiabetic patients. 54,55 Moreover, the average reduction in the rate of decline in eGFR observed in DAPA-CKD and EMPA-KIDNEY exceeded that reported in SELECT, suggesting that the renoprotective mechanisms of SGLT2 inhibitors may provide a more direct and potent impact on kidney disease progression. In SELECT, semaglutide reduced the annual rate of eGFR decline by approximately 1.08 mL/min/1.73 m 2 compared with placebo (-1.07 vs -2.15 mL/min/1.73 m 2 /y). 49 In DAPA-CKD, dapagliflozin slowed eGFR decline by an average of 1.65 mL/min/1.73 m 2 /y (-2.86 with placebo vs -1.21 with dapagliflozin), and in EMPA-KIDNEY, empagliflozin slowed the annual eGFR decline by 1.36 mL/min/1.73 m 2 /y (-2.74 with placebo vs -1.38 with empagliflozin). 54,55 These larger differences in eGFR slope with SGLT2 inhibitors support a potentially more potent impact on long-term kidney function preservation when compared to GLP-1 receptor agonists. Although indirect comparisons must be interpreted with caution due to differences in trial populations and endpoints, the current body of evidence suggests that SGLT2 inhibitors may exert a more pronounced effect on kidney outcomes than GLP-1 receptor agonists. However, given their complementary mechanisms of action, GLP-1 receptor agonists may still play an important role, especially in patients with obesity, atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, or intolerance to SGLT2 inhibitors. Although SGLT2 inhibitors appear to yield greater reductions in hard renal outcomes, their mechanisms differ from those of GLP-1 receptor agonists, raising the possibility of synergistic benefit.

### p4

The complementary mechanisms of SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists suggest potential additive or synergistic renoprotective effects. SGLT2 inhibitors confer renal benefit primarily through hemodynamic modulation reducing intraglomerular pressure via tubuloglomerular feedback along with improved natriuresis, reduced inflammation, and enhanced mitochondrial efficiency. In contrast, GLP-1 receptor agonists exert effects mainly through anti-inflammatory, antifibrotic, and metabolic pathways. Preclinical models support this mechanistic synergy: a 2024 animal study published in Frontiers in Pharmacology demonstrated that combined therapy with an SGLT2 inhibitor and a GLP-1 receptor agonist produced greater reductions in oxidative stress, tubular injury, and fibrosis compared with monotherapy. 62 Together, these findings reinforce the biological plausibility of dual therapy and provide a rationale for clinical trials evaluating combination strategies to maximize kidney and cardiovascular protection.

### p5

The dual therapy of dapagliflozin (SGLT2 inhibitor) and liraglutide almost preserved renal function in a diabetesinduced renal injury animal model, as evident by the restoration of serum creatinine, serum urea, and proteinuria levels. Interestingly, the combined dapagliflozin and liraglutide treatment provided more renoprotective effects compared with the use of either drug class alone. Some of the explained mechanisms include reduction of inflammatory mediators (TNF-α, NF-κB), apoptotic markers (cleaved caspase 3), and oxidative stress. 63 In agreement with the aforementioned study, Martos-Guillami et al 62 demonstrated the synergistic cardiorenal protective effects of empagliflozin (SGLT2 inhibitor) and semaglutide (GLP-1 receptor agonist) in an animal model of diabetic kidney disease. They showed that the use of triple therapy, empagliflozin and semaglutide with an ACE inhibitor, which is the first-line treatment for patients with CKD, reduced blood glucose levels, glomerular mesangial matrix expansion, and profibrotic gene expression more effectively than either drug class alone with ACE inhibition. Taken together, preclinical studies suggest that the combination of SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists may provide synergistic renoprotective effects, highlighting a promising strategy for managing CKD.

### p6

Importantly, subgroup analyses from FLOW offer preliminary insight into the potential for additive benefit when GLP-1 receptor agonists are used alongside SGLT2 inhibitors. Although the trial was not powered to detect significant differences within these subgroups, the renal benefits of semaglutide appeared consistent regardless of SGLT2 inhibitor use at baseline. There was no evidence of harm and no attenuation of effect, supporting the biological plausibility of complementary mechanisms. 50 These exploratory findings raise the hypothesis that GLP-1 receptor agonists could provide incremental benefit when layered onto standard therapy, including RAAS and SGLT2 inhibition. Given their distinct mechanisms-GLP-1 receptor agonists acting largely through metabolic and antiinflammatory pathways and SGLT2 inhibitors via hemodynamic and tubular effects-dual therapy may offer synergistic renoprotection. However, dedicated randomized trials are needed to rigorously evaluate this hypothesis. Until then, GLP-1 receptor agonists represent a promising adjunctive strategy in patients with CKD, particularly those with obesity, cardiovascular disease, or intolerance to SGLT2 inhibitors.

### p7

CKD represents a heterogeneous spectrum of pathologies that extends beyond diabetic kidney disease. Nondiabetic CKD includes diverse etiologies such as glomerulonephritis, interstitial nephritis, toxin-induced kidney injury, and hereditary conditions such as autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease. The extent to which GLP-1 receptor agonists confer renoprotective benefits across these varied subtypes remains unclear. Current evidence is primarily derived from populations with diabetic or obesity-associated CKD, which may limit extrapolation to other forms of kidney disease. Future studies designed to evaluate subgroup-specific outcomes will be essential to clarify whether the magnitude of benefit differs according to CKD etiology, disease stage, or comorbid cardiovascular risk profile.

### p8

An additional consideration when interpreting the kidney-specific effects of GLP-1 receptor agonists is the background use of established nephroprotective therapies in the major clinical trials. In SELECT, fewer than 7% of the 17,604 participants were receiving SGLT2 inhibitors at baseline, and approximately 75% were on RAAS inhibitors, such as ACE inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockersreflecting contemporary standard-of-care but also indicating that the renal benefits observed with semaglutide 2.4 mg weekly occurred largely independent of SGLT2 inhibitor cotherapy. 49 Similarly, in FLOW, about 10% of the 3,533 participants were receiving SGLT2 inhibitors while over 85% were on background RAAS inhibition. 50 This high use of RAAS blockers paired with low SGLT2 inhibitor utilization suggests that the observed improvements in eGFR decline and composite kidney outcomes with semaglutide were primarily attributable to the GLP-1 receptor agonist itself, in the setting of already optimized RAAS blockade.

## Meier et al. (2026): CONCLUSION


### p1

Although the role of GLP-1 receptor agonists in nondiabetic CKD is not yet fully defined, their distinct mechanismstargeting metabolic dysfunction, inflammation, and excess weight-suggest they may complement rather than duplicate existing therapies. For patients with obesity, atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, or intolerance to SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP-1 receptor agonists may offer a valuable alternative. Future studies are essential to evaluate whether dual therapy provides additive or synergistic benefits and to clarify optimal sequencing or combination strategies across diverse patient populations.
